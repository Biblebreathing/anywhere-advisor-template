# Fact Sheet — Estate · Colorado · 2026

Every number cites a fact ID from `fact-registry.md`. Every Colorado legal fact is `[ATTORNEY REVIEW]` before publication. Educational only, not legal advice.

## 1. The core problem, in one paragraph
Most Americans have no will. Caring.com's 2025 survey puts it at about 1 in 4 adults [F-053]; Gallup's older poll found 3 in 4 people 65+ have one but only about half of 50–64-year-olds do [F-054]. Greg's prospects (60–68, often a couple with an IRA-heavy balance sheet and a home worth well above the national median [F-076]) are more likely to have *old* documents than none. In Colorado the big risk usually isn't a state death tax (there isn't one [F-040]), and a federal estate tax applies only above $15 million per person in 2026 [F-038]. The real costs are delay, court process and incapacity. Probate has built-in waiting periods (a 4-month creditor window, at least 6 months before an informal closing) [F-044–F-046], and without durable powers of attorney a family may have no one with authority to act if a spouse can't [F-050–F-052]. Greg's "One Year and One Day" story (KB §6) is exactly this problem.

## 4. Myths vs. facts
| What people believe | What's actually true | Fact ID |
|---|---|---|
| "Colorado will take a cut when I die." | Colorado has no estate tax and no inheritance tax. | F-040 |
| "We'll owe estate tax." | The federal exemption is $15 million per person for 2026 deaths. Very few of Greg's $1M–$4M households are near it. | F-038 |
| "A will keeps my family out of probate." | A will is filed with the court and directs the probate process. Colorado requires it to be lodged within 10 days of death. Non-probate tools (beneficiary designations, beneficiary deeds, trusts) are what avoid probate for those assets. | F-047, F-048 |
| "If I name my kids on the deed now, that's the same as a beneficiary deed." | A beneficiary deed gives the beneficiary nothing during your life; you keep full control and can revoke it. *(Gifting a current interest has different consequences; attorney to explain.)* | F-048, F-049 |
| "My will can change who gets the house." | Not if a valid beneficiary deed is recorded. A will cannot revoke or change it. | F-049 |
| "Small estates are easy, so we don't need a plan." | The affidavit shortcut covers only personal property under $88,000 (2026). Almost any home in Larimer County (median $610,000) is far above it, and real estate generally isn't covered. | F-042, F-076 |
| "My spouse can just sign for me if I'm incapacitated." | Not automatically. A durable financial power of attorney is what gives an agent authority. In Colorado, POAs made since 2010 are durable unless they say otherwise. | F-051 |
| "Most people my age have this handled." | About half of Americans 50–64 had a will in 2021 (Gallup), and only 24% of adults overall had one in Caring.com's 2025 survey. | F-053, F-054 |

## 6. Recent and upcoming changes
- **Federal exemption:** OBBBA set $15M for 2026, indexed for inflation from 2027 [F-038]. (What prior law would have done in 2026 was not sourced in this run; don't cite a "sunset" figure without adding a fact ID.)
- **Colorado small-estate limit** rises every year with the cost of living ($82K for 2024 deaths, $86K for 2025, $88K for 2026) [F-042]. Refresh every January.
- **Federal Medicaid home-equity cap** of $1M from 2028 [F-091] (relevant to LTC and estate conversations) `[ATTORNEY REVIEW]`.
