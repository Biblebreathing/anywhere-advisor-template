## A. Claims assigned for this stage

### (a) "A new tax law" made commercial real estate development "a ghost town" (KB §2, §6)
**Status: Confirmed in substance; reword the year and the "ghost town" image.**
- The law fits the **Tax Reform Act of 1986**. The FDIC's history of the period says TRA86 eliminated accelerated depreciation (ACRS) and the ability to use passive real estate losses against other income, and that this "dampen[ed] the demand for commercial real estate investments during the late 1980s and early 1990s" and "helped soften real estate prices" [F-092]. Real estate limited partnership fundraising fell from $16 billion (1985) to $1.5 billion (1989) [F-093]. Depreciation went from 15-year accelerated to 31.5-year straight-line [F-094].
- **Caveat:** FDIC also points to massive overbuilding (office vacancy rose from 4.9% in 1980 to 18.9% in 1991), the 1990–91 recession, and bank and thrift failures [F-095]. The tax law was a major cause, not the only one.
- **Timing:** TRA86's effects ran into the early 1990s [F-092], so a graduation around 1987–1993 is consistent with the story. "33 years ago" (≈1993) is plausible. But a third-party listing says Balanced Financial has operated "since 1987" [F-101], which conflicts with both 27 and 33 years. See `tensions.md` #1.
- **Safe wording:** "When I graduated, the 1986 tax reform had just pulled the rug out from under commercial real estate. Investors lost the tax write-offs that made projects pencil out, and development dried up. I learned early that a change in tax law can wipe out a plan you didn't make." (Keep "ghost town" as his memory: "it felt like a ghost town.")

### (d) CEA (Certified Estate Advisor) and CSA (Certified Senior Advisor): issuer, FINRA listing, accreditation
**CSA: Confirmed listed; accreditation per issuer only.**
- Issuer: Society of Certified Senior Advisors [F-097].
- FINRA's Professional Designations database lists the CSA as "currently offered and recognized by the issuing organization": no prerequisites, a proctored exam, 30 hours of CE every three years, and a public sanctions list [F-097].
- Accreditation: the Society says the CSA is accredited by **ANAB and NCCA** [F-098]. FINRA's CSA page shows no accreditation field, and we did not confirm it in the ANAB/NCCA directories. **Action:** pull the ANAB and NCCA directory entries and file them before Greg uses "CSA."
- **Why it matters:** FINRA notes that some state regulators allow senior designations only if ANAB- or NCCA-accredited; the NASAA model rule gives a safe harbor for accredited designations; FINRA Regulatory Notice 11-52 covers supervision [F-100]. Colorado's insurance rule on senior designations (Reg. 1-2-18) exists, but its text wasn't fetched [F-100]. Greg must also be a current CSA in good standing (intake says only "one signature shows CSA").

**CEA: Can't verify; hold off using it in marketing.**
- **"Certified Estate Advisor" does not appear in FINRA's designation database**, under either the name or the abbreviation CEA [F-099].
- Similar names that are listed: Certified Estate and Trust Specialist (CES, IBF), Certified Estate Planner (CEP), Certified Trust & Estate Wealth Advisor (CTEWA, WealthCounsel), and Accredited Estate Planner (AEP, NAEPC). A Canadian "Certified Executor Advisor (CEA)" also exists [F-099].
- **Action (compliance):** ask Greg for the issuing organization, the certificate, CE status, and whether it is accredited. Until then, leave "CEA" out of new assets, or have the CCO/FIG approve it in writing. FINRA's listing doesn't imply endorsement in either case [F-097].
