## Slide 20: Four Roads Your Assets Can Take
*Act 2 · cards · angle: method*

"Think of everything you own as going down one of four roads when you die."

"Road one is the will. Your will doesn't avoid the court. It's the instruction manual for the court. In Colorado, it has to be filed with the district court within ten days of death. Anything that only has the will telling it where to go, goes through probate."

"Road two is beneficiary forms. Your IRA, your 401(k), your life insurance. Those generally go to whoever is named on the form, not whoever is named in your will. That's powerful. And it's dangerous if the form is twenty years old."

"Road three is one a lot of Colorado homeowners have never heard of. It's called a beneficiary deed. You sign it and record it while you're alive. You keep full control of the house. You can change it or revoke it. And at your death, the house passes to whoever you named. Your will can't override it. If you own rentals, all those tenants and toilets, this is worth asking your attorney about."

"Road four is a funded trust. A living trust only controls what's actually titled in its name. The trust is the bucket. Funding is putting the water in."

"Grab your workbook, page two. Next to each of your big assets, write which road it's on today: will, beneficiary form, deed, or trust. If you don't know, write a question mark. Question marks are fine. That's what the page is for."

## Slide 26: Four Things That Drift
*Act 2 · cards · angle: method*

"Four things drift. Here they are."

"One, beneficiaries. Every retirement account and life insurance policy has a beneficiary form, and those forms generally control where that money goes, ahead of the will. So after a marriage, a divorce, a death, or a new grandchild, you check every single one. A form you filled out twenty years ago can send money somewhere you'd never choose today."

"Two, trust funding. A trust only controls what's titled in it. Every time you open a new account, refinance, or buy a property, somebody has to ask: is this in the trust, or does it go around it?"

"Three, special needs. If someone you love receives needs-based benefits, leaving them money outright can cause problems with those benefits. Attorneys have tools for this, like a special-needs trust. But the beneficiary forms and the accounts have to point to it. Otherwise the plan says one thing and the money does another."

"Four, blended families. If you've remarried, 'everything to my spouse' can end up meaning your kids from a first marriage get nothing, if your spouse later changes their own plan. That's not a moral question. It's a drafting question and a beneficiary question, and it's one to raise with your attorney."
