### 2. Day 1
**Subject:** Should I do a Roth conversion?
*[Swap in the most common question from the live chat if it was different; get the answer approved.]*
**Preview:** The honest answer, and how to get yours

Hi {{first_name}},

A question that comes up whenever we talk about taxes: "Should I do a Roth conversion?"

The honest answer is: it depends, and anyone who answers it without your tax return is guessing. Here's what it depends on:

1. **Your rate now vs. your likely rate later.** A converted amount is taxed as ordinary income in the year you convert, and you can't undo it.
2. **Your Medicare line.** Medicare uses your income from two years earlier. In 2026, the first surcharge for a married couple starts above $218,000 of income.
3. **Where the money for the tax comes from.**
4. **The five-year rules.** A qualified Roth withdrawal isn't counted as income under current law, but only after age 59½ *and* five tax years after your first Roth IRA contribution. Each conversion also has its own five-year clock for the 10% additional tax.

Sometimes converting makes sense. Sometimes it doesn't. Two plus two equals four, but only with your numbers.

### 3. Day 3
**Subject:** When the Millers became one
**Preview:** A hypothetical, and the part nobody plans for

Hi {{first_name}},

On the webinar I told the story of the Millers. They're a hypothetical couple, not real clients, but the numbers are real 2026 figures.

Both engineers. A big IRA. Retired, with required withdrawals, Social Security, and some dividends on one return.

Then Mr. Miller passes away. For that year, Mrs. Miller can still file jointly. After that, without a dependent child at home, she files single. Her income doesn't fall by half: the IRA and the dividends are still there. But her brackets do. In 2026, the 24% bracket starts at $211,400 of taxable income for a married couple and at $105,700 for a single filer. The first Medicare surcharge starts above $218,000 for a couple and above $109,000 for one person.

The person who's grieving can land in higher brackets on roughly the same income. That's why a tax plan has to work for both of you, joint and single.

### 2. Day 1
**Subject:** The 3 things I covered
**Preview:** In case you only have five minutes

Hi {{first_name}},

If you don't have an hour for the replay, here are the three things I covered:

- **Why "I'll be in a lower bracket when I retire" can backfire.** For people with large pre-tax savings, required withdrawals (starting at 75 if you were born in 1960 or later) can keep retirement income near working-year levels.
- **How RMDs, Social Security, Medicare surcharges, and RSUs stack up on one return.** Up to 85% of Social Security can be taxable, and Medicare sets its surcharges using your income from two years earlier.
- **The planning window before your RMDs begin.** Roth conversions, RSU timing, and the year you turn 63 (which can set your Medicare premium at 65) are all decisions you can make on purpose.
